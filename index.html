A gallery on Elizabeth Bishop's

One Art

An immersive exhibition on loss

The art of losing isn't hard to master;
so many things seem filled with the intent
to be lost that their loss is no disaster.

Lose something every day. Accept the fluster
of lost door keys, the hour badly spent.
The art of losing isn't hard to master.

Then practice losing farther, losing faster:
places, and names, and where it was you meant
to travel. None of these will bring disaster.

I lost my mother's watch. And look! my last, or
next-to-last, of three loved houses went.
The art of losing isn't hard to master.

I lost two cities, lovely ones. And, vaster,
some realms I owned, two rivers, a continent.
I miss them, but it wasn't a disaster.

—Even losing you (the joking voice, a gesture
I love) I shan't have lied. It's evident
the art of losing's not too hard to master
though it may look like (Write it!) like disaster.

— Elizabeth Bishop, 1976

Object

A Ring of Mismatched Keys

Keys to unknown doors, belonging to unknown houses. We carry what we no longer need access to.

"lost door keys, the hour badly spent"

📋

Document

A To-Do List, Mostly Uncrossed

Tasks that were meant. Errands half-run. The quiet archaeology of an ordinary afternoon that went nowhere.

"the hour badly spent"

🎟️

Object / Text

An Unused Train Ticket

Dated, stamped, and never torn. A journey intended — to a place the speaker once meant to go.

"where it was you meant / to travel"

Room II

"I lost my mother's watch."

⌚

Heirloom / Object

A Pocket Watch, Stopped

The watch Bishop lost was her mother's — one of the most intimate objects in the poem. A pocket watch stopped at an arbitrary hour becomes every hour the mother's hands ever touched. Its loss marks the first rupture in the poem's facade of composure.

"I lost my mother's watch."

🏡 🏡 🏡

Photograph

Three Houses — Only Outlines Remain

Bishop lived in many houses across her life: in Nova Scotia, Key West, Brazil. She lost them all, one by one, to time or exile or grief.

"my last, or / next-to-last, of three loved houses went"

📖

Manuscript

Bishop's Draft — One of Seventeen

The Vassar College archive holds seventeen drafts of this poem. In early versions, the final stanza breaks down entirely. The controlled form was hard-won — the effort to master it visible in every crossed-out line.

"(Write it!)"

Room III

"I lost two cities, lovely ones. And, vaster,
some realms I owned, two rivers, a continent."

🗺️

Cartography

Antique Map of Brazil

Bishop lived in Petrópolis and Rio de Janeiro for fifteen years, following her partner Lota de Macedo Soares. When Lota died in 1967, Bishop lost not just a person — she lost an entire geography of self.

"a continent"

🧭

Object

A Compass Without a True North

To lose a continent is to lose orientation itself. The compass points somewhere — but where you meant to go no longer exists in the way you knew it.

"places, and names, and where it was you meant / to travel"

🌊

Photograph

The Amazon & the Charles — Two Rivers

Critics identify these as the Amazon (Brazil) and the Charles (Cambridge, MA, where Bishop later taught). Two rivers, two phases of a life that could not be reconciled.

"two rivers"

✉️

Published Text

Bishop's Letters to Robert Lowell

Their decades-long correspondence traces the losses Bishop refused to name directly in her poetry. Here, in prose, the disaster is legible between the lines.

"I miss them, but it wasn't a disaster."

Photograph

A Blurred Portrait

The face partially obscured. The "you" of the poem is never named — Lota, most likely. Or the idea of Lota. Or everyone.

"Even losing you"

📝

Object

An Unfinished Letter

Signed, sealed, unsent. What do you say to someone you've lost? The letter waits for an address that no longer exists.

"the joking voice, a gesture / I love"

☕

Object

A Cup, A Lipstick Mark

Nothing survives grief like the mundane. A stained cup is more unbearable than a monument — because the monument was never real, and the cup was.

"a gesture / I love"
